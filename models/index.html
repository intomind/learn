Models that learn

How can a computer learn a rule that nobody told it?

Learning from examples

A model is a set of rules a computer learns from examples. Each example comes with its answer, called its label. Show a model many pictures of animals labeled cat or dog, and it can learn what makes cats and dogs different. Then it can decide about animals it has never seen.

Nobody writes the rule for it. The rule comes from the examples.

Testing on new examples

A model that only remembers the examples it was shown isn't much use. It answers those perfectly, and is stuck on anything new.

So a model is always tested on examples it did not learn from. Training fits its rules to examples whose answers are known. Testing checks them on examples held back.

A line between two groups

The simplest useful classifier draws a line between two groups, or with more features, a flat plane. Each example gets a score: a weighted sum of its features, plus a constant. The weights are fitted so the scores come close to 0 for one group and 1 for the other, and everything above 0.5 is called the second group.

The usual way to fit them is least squares: choose the weights that make the squared differences between the scores and the labels as small as possible. The Mind-Controlled Light Switch does exactly this, with 76 features.

Overfitting

A rule flexible enough to fit every training example, noise and all, overfits: it scores well on what it saw, and worse on anything new. The gap between the training score and the held-out score measures it.

So a model is scored on examples it never learned from. The Mind-Controlled Light Switch teaches in eight rounds, and tests each round with a head taught on the other seven.

Least squares

Stack the examples as the rows of X, with a column of ones for the constant. Least squares chooses the weights that minimize the squared error ‖Xw − y‖², and setting the gradient to zero gives the normal equations:

w = (XᵀX)⁻¹ Xᵀy

Training fits w on some examples. Testing scores it on others it never saw, which estimates how it will do on new ones: training and testing.

Try it

A line through examples

Fit the line on the first sixteen points by the normal equations, and score it on those and on the eight held back.

Too many features, and ridge regression

As the number of features d approaches the number of examples n, some combinations of features are barely determined by the data. XᵀX gets tiny eigenvalues, their inverses blow up the weights, and the fit chases noise: overfitting. The Mind-Controlled Light Switch fits 77 parameters to 88 readings, squarely in that regime.

Ridge regression adds a penalty λ on the squared weights, leaving the constant unpenalized:

w = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹ Xᵀy

Every eigenvalue rises by λ, which bounds the inverse. A larger λ shrinks the weights, adding bias and removing variance.

Choosing λ without the test

The best λ minimizes error on data the fit hasn't seen, but choosing it by its score on the test fits λ to the test, and flatters that score. So λ is chosen inside the training data, by cross-validation nested inside each training set, or by a rule that uses no labels at all.

The demos' trainer uses such a rule: the geometric mean of the two smallest eigenvalues of XᵀX for the centered embeddings. Embeddings barely vary along one direction, and a penalty of that size removes it while leaving the rest almost unshrunk.

Try it

The ridge path

The demos' source holds the trainer's test data: 150 rows of 76 numbers in tests/fixtures/train/embeddings.json, with labels in targets.json. They aren't EEG: they are random numbers shaped like embeddings, labeled by a rule with noise, and stored as the integers the IntoMind One sends, so λ is in those units. From the top of the demos' source, trace training and held-out error against λ, holding out blocks of consecutive rows, then choose λ by nested cross-validation.

import json
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

X = np.array(json.load(open("tests/fixtures/train/embeddings.json")), float)
y = np.array(json.load(open("tests/fixtures/train/targets.json")), float)

def fit(X, y, lam):
    """Ridge on the weights, none on the intercept."""
    A = np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))]
    P = lam * np.eye(A.shape[1])
    P[-1, -1] = 0
    return np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + P, A.T @ y)

def mse(X, y, w):
    return np.mean((np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))] @ w - y) ** 2)

def blocked(X, y, lam, k=5):
    """Training and held-out error, holding out k blocks of consecutive rows in turn."""
    edges = np.linspace(0, len(X), k + 1).astype(int)
    train, held = [], []
    for a, b in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]):
        out = np.zeros(len(X), bool)
        out[a:b] = True
        w = fit(X[~out], y[~out], lam)
        train.append(mse(X[~out], y[~out], w))
        held.append(mse(X[out], y[out], w))
    return np.mean(train), np.mean(held)

lams = 10.0 ** np.arange(4, 13)
errors = np.array([blocked(X, y, lam) for lam in lams])
plt.semilogx(lams, errors[:, 0], label="training")
plt.semilogx(lams, errors[:, 1], label="held out")
plt.xlabel("λ")
plt.ylabel("mean squared error")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

# Nested: each outer block's λ is chosen inside the other blocks only.
edges = np.linspace(0, len(X), 6).astype(int)
for a, b in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]):
    out = np.zeros(len(X), bool)
    out[a:b] = True
    best = min(lams, key=lambda lam: blocked(X[~out], y[~out], lam)[1])
    w = fit(X[~out], y[~out], best)
    print(f"rows {a} to {b}: λ {best:.0e}, held-out error {mse(X[out], y[out], w):.3f}")
print("the trainer's λ:", round(json.load(open("tests/fixtures/train/ridge.json"))["ridge"]))

You should seeTraining error rising as λ grows, and held-out error falling to its lowest near 10⁸ to 10⁹, then rising. Nested cross-validation chooses λ there in every block, thousands of times the trainer's λ of about 20,000.

Chapter 5 · Graduate

Models that learn

How do you fit a decoder with nearly as many weights as examples, what does the penalty you choose do to it and to its test, and what do its weights say about the data?

Linear decoders

For two classes with means μ0 and μ1 and a shared covariance, Fisher's discriminant (1936) projects each example x onto

w ∝ SW−1(μ1 − μ0)

where SW is the pooled within-class scatter. Least squares on labels of 0 and 1 finds the same direction: the total scatter is the within-class scatter plus a term along μ1 − μ0, and inverting it leaves the direction unchanged (Duda, Hart and Stork, 2001). Logistic regression fits the same family by likelihood. All three need SW or its like, and with 76 features and 88 windows, as in the Mind-Controlled Light Switch, its smallest eigenvalues are estimated far too small and its inverse is mostly noise.

Ridge and shrinkage

Ridge regression adds a penalty λ on the weights' squared size, with the intercept left free (Hoerl and Kennard, 1970). On centered data Xc,

ŵ = (XcᵀXc + λI)−1 Xcᵀy

In the eigenbasis of XcᵀXc, with eigenvalues dj, ridge keeps a fraction dj/(dj + λ) of the least-squares fit along each direction, and the effective degrees of freedom are Σ dj/(dj + λ) (Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, 2009). On labels, ridge is the discriminant with SW + λI in place of SW.

Shrinkage LDA replaces the covariance with (1 − γ)Σ̂ + γνI, where ν is its mean eigenvalue and γ is set analytically from the data (Ledoit and Wolf, 2004). It is the standard decoder for EEG with few trials (Blankertz and others, 2011).

Choosing the penalty, honestly

A penalty chosen by cross-validation is fitted to the data like any weight, and the cross-validated score at the chosen penalty is optimistic. Nested cross-validation chooses the penalty inside each outer training set and scores the outer test set with it (Varma and Simon, 2006). Selection overfits too, and the more settings are compared, the more it does (Cawley and Talbot, 2010). With windows from one session, every fold must also respect time, which is the next chapter's subject.

Too little penalty has its own peak. As the number of training examples approaches the number of weights, an unpenalized fit's variance grows without bound, and its error on new data peaks: the top of the curve Belkin and others (2019) called double descent, analyzed for least squares by Hastie and others (2022). A training set of 77 windows for 76 weights sits right on it.

What the weights mean

A decoder's weights are a filter: they combine features to recover the label, and they weigh a feature heavily as readily to cancel noise it shares with another as to pick up signal. The activation pattern, a ∝ Σxw, says how the decoded quantity shows in each feature, and only the pattern can be read as where the signal is (Haufe and others, 2014). On a pretrained encoder's embedding, neither maps to the cortex: the features are learned, and each mixes everything the encoder kept.

In the demo

The Mind-Controlled Light Switch fits ridge regression of labels 0 and 1 on the device's embeddings as integers, each value times 4,096 and rounded, with the intercept unpenalized. It refuses to fit unless there are more windows than the embedding's 76 numbers. No label enters its penalty: λ is the geometric mean of the two smallest eigenvalues of the centered Gram matrix, the smallest floored at 1. The device's embeddings, like the trainer's test fixture, sum to about zero, so one direction, every feature equal, carries almost no variance. The smallest eigenvalue is that direction's, and the penalty sits between it and the next, suppressing it and barely touching the rest.

Analysis

Analysis

The trainer's penalty, and better ones

In the demos' source, the trainer's fixture holds 150 windows of embeddings as integers, balanced labels, and the penalty the Python API chose for them. Reproduce the penalty, then compare it with others by contiguous five-fold cross-validation, choose one by nested cross-validation, and fit shrinkage LDA. Then cut the data to the light switch's size.

import json
import numpy as np
from sklearn.discriminant_analysis import LinearDiscriminantAnalysis

F = "tests/fixtures/train/"                  # in the demos' source
X = np.array(json.load(open(F + "embeddings.json")), float)   # 150 windows of 76, as integers
y = np.array(json.load(open(F + "targets.json")), float)      # 0 and 1
trainer = json.load(open(F + "ridge.json"))["ridge"]

def gap_ridge(X):
    """The demo's penalty: the geometric mean of the two smallest eigenvalues."""
    ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh((X - X.mean(axis=0)).T @ (X - X.mean(axis=0)))
    return np.sqrt(max(ev[0], 1) * ev[1])

Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)
ev, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(Xc.T @ Xc)                          # ascending
gap = gap_ridge(X)
print(f"smallest eigenvalues {ev[0]:.2f} and {ev[1]:.3g}: "
      f"gap ridge {gap:.2f}, the trainer's {trainer:.2f}")
cosine = abs(vecs[:, 0].sum()) / 76 ** 0.5
print(f"the smallest one's direction against all ones: cosine {cosine:.3f}")

def fit(X, y, lam):
    """Ridge with the intercept left unpenalized, as the demo's fitLinear does."""
    m = X.mean(axis=0)
    xc = X - m
    w = np.linalg.solve(xc.T @ xc + lam * np.eye(X.shape[1]), xc.T @ (y - y.mean()))
    return w, y.mean() - m @ w

def blocks(n, k=5):
    size = -(-n // k)
    return [np.arange(i * size, min(n, (i + 1) * size)) for i in range(k)]

def blocked(X, y, lam, k=5):
    """Accuracy and squared error on contiguous held-out blocks: a score above 0.5 means 1.
    lam="gap" chooses the demo's penalty afresh from each training set."""
    hits = err = 0.0
    for test in blocks(len(y), k):
        train = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(y)), test)
        penalty = gap_ridge(X[train]) if lam == "gap" else lam
        w, b = fit(X[train], y[train], penalty)
        s = X[test] @ w + b
        hits += np.sum((s > 0.5) == (y[test] == 1))
        err += np.sum((s - y[test]) ** 2)
    return hits / len(y), err / len(y)

grid = [10.0 ** p for p in range(5, 11)]
for lam in [gap] + grid:
    acc, mse = blocked(X, y, lam)
    print(f"lambda {lam:8.2g}  effective df {np.sum(ev / (ev + lam)):5.1f}  "
          f"accuracy {acc:.2f}  mse {mse:.3f}")

# Nested: choose lambda inside each outer training set, then score the outer block.
hits, chosen = 0, []
for test in blocks(len(y)):
    train = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(y)), test)
    best = min(grid, key=lambda lam: blocked(X[train], y[train], lam, k=4)[1])
    w, b = fit(X[train], y[train], best)
    hits += np.sum((X[test] @ w + b > 0.5) == (y[test] == 1))
    chosen.append(f"{best:.0e}")
print(f"nested: accuracy {hits / len(y):.2f}, lambdas chosen {chosen}")

# Shrinkage LDA, its shrinkage set analytically from each training set (Ledoit and Wolf).
hits = 0
for test in blocks(len(y)):
    train = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(y)), test)
    lda = LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(solver="lsqr", shrinkage="auto").fit(X[train], y[train])
    hits += np.sum(lda.predict(X[test]) == y[test])
print(f"shrinkage LDA: accuracy {hits / len(y):.2f}")

# Cut to the light switch's size: 88 windows held out a round of 11 at a time, so every
# training set has 77 windows for 76 weights.
for lam in ("gap", 1e8, 1e9):
    acc = blocked(X[:88], y[:88], lam, k=8)[0]
    name = lam if lam == "gap" else f"{lam:.0e}"
    print(f"88 windows in 8 folds, lambda {name}: accuracy {acc:.2f}")

You should seeThe trainer's penalty reproduced exactly, 20,131.58, between eigenvalues of 5.07 and 7.99 × 10⁷, and the smallest one's direction all ones. At that penalty the fit spends 75 of its 76 degrees of freedom and scores 0.67. Squared error is lowest near 10⁹, at 31 degrees of freedom and 0.70. Nested cross-validation chooses 10⁸ or 10⁹ and scores 0.71, and shrinkage LDA 0.69. Cut to 88 windows held out a round of 11 at a time, the trainer's penalty scores 0.50, chance, where 10⁸ scores 0.61 and 10⁹ 0.66.

Open problems

  1. Small data. With the few trials of a calibration session, linear decoders with shrinkage remain hard to beat, and whether more flexible models help depends on how much data a person can give (Lotte and others, 2018).
  2. Reading a head. Patterns in a pretrained embedding don't map to sources, and how to say what a head on such an embedding responds to is open.
  3. The penalty over time. The best penalty for one session needn't be the best for the next, when the data have shifted. How to regularize for the session to come rather than the one at hand is unsettled.

Problems

  1. Show that least squares on labels of 0 and 1 gives a weight vector parallel to Fisher's discriminant.

  2. How much of the least-squares fit does ridge keep along a direction whose eigenvalue equals λ? Along one ten times larger, and ten times smaller?

  3. The demo's penalty uses no labels. Why does that matter for its test against chance?

  4. A decoder puts its largest weight on feature 3. Can you conclude that feature 3 carries the signal? What would you compute instead?

  5. The light switch keeps 88 windows in eight rounds of 11. Its test holds out one round at a time. How many windows does each training set have, and why does the trainer refuse fewer than 77?
