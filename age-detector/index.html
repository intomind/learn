Age Detector

An AI model on the IntoMind One estimates your age from your EEG. How good is its guess, really?

Get ready

  • A computer with Chrome or Edge

  • A slip of paper for each person

What happened

This model learned from the EEG of thousands of people whose ages were known. It looks at four seconds of your EEG at a time, and makes a guess. The number on the screen is the average of its guesses so far, which is why it settled.

Every guess can be wrong. When the model was tested on people it had never learned from, it was off by about nine years on average. That is its error, and it is what “± 9 years” means.

Guessing that everyone is the class's most common age is a baseline: something simple to compare the model against. In a class of people the same age, the baseline wins. The number doesn't tell you how old your brain is. It shows that this model can find a little information about age in EEG, with a large error.

Talk about it

  1. The screen said ± 9 years. What does that mean?

  2. Your class is all about the same age. Who guesses better: the model, or someone who says everyone is the class's age? Why?

  3. Does the number tell you how old your brain is?

Lab 3 · High school

Age Detector

An AI model on the IntoMind One estimates your age from your EEG. How big is its error, and is it any better than a guess that ignores your brain completely?

Background

Read it the night before.

A model on the chip

The IntoMind One's encoder sums up each four seconds of EEG as 76 numbers, its embedding, and a small model, the age head, turns those 76 numbers into an age. The head learned from the EEG of 3,742 people of known age, in 93 studies.

The demo shows the average of the head's estimates so far, one for each four-second window, so the number settles as more windows come in.

Its error, and what to compare it with

The head was tested on whole studies it had never seen. Averaging a person's windows, its error was 9.16 years on average: that is the “± 9 years” on the screen.

Whether that is good depends on what you compare it with. A baseline that ignores the brain completely, always guessing the average age of the people the head learned from, was off by 9.77 years.

Where it goes, and why

Anywhere. The encoder reads its four seconds of EEG without being told where the electrodes are, and the head was scored across studies that placed their electrodes differently, so the published error already includes that variety. The top of the head, Cz, is on the midline and easy to find, and the back, Oz, and the forehead, Fpz, work too.

Lab 3 · College

Age Detector

The age head is said to read any site on the head. Does moving the IntoMind One change one person's estimate by more than the spread of its own windows, and in this room, does the head beat guessing the room's most common age?

Background

Read it before the lab.

What runs, and where

Every four seconds the IntoMind One's encoder turns the EEG into 76 numbers, and the age head, built into the model and selected from power on, turns them into years. The demo averages the estimates, leaving out windows with a gap in the signal, and shows the mean with the published error, ± 9 years.

The encoder takes no electrode positions, and the head was scored with the positions hidden, on montages of 2, 4 and 8 electrodes. So it should read any site: a claim you can test on one person.

What the evaluation says

Averaged over a person's windows, the head misses by 9.16 years on studies it never saw, against 9.77 for always guessing the training studies' mean age: 0.61 years better, with a 95 percent interval from 0.03 worse to 1.25 better. It isn't clearly better than a guess that ignores the brain, and its estimates are pulled toward that mean.

In one room, the fair guess is the room's own most common age, and it can do far better.

Comparing two sites fairly

Neighboring windows are alike, so thirty windows aren't thirty independent estimates. Average them in blocks of five, and compare the two sites' blocks with a permutation test: every way of splitting the blocks into two sites, scored the same way.

Analysis

Read each saved run, compare the two sites in blocks of five windows, and test the difference by permuting the blocks.

import re
from itertools import combinations
import numpy as np

def estimates(path):
    """Each window's estimate from a saved run of age.py, leaving out the flagged windows."""
    lines = [l for l in open(path) if l.startswith("window ") and "kept out" not in l]
    return np.array([float(re.search(r": ([\d.]+) years", l).group(1)) for l in lines])

def blocks(x, size=5):
    """Means of consecutive blocks of windows: neighboring windows are alike."""
    n = len(x) // size
    return x[:n * size].reshape(n, size).mean(axis=1)

first, second = estimates("w1-first.txt"), estimates("w1-second.txt")   # the runs you saved
for name, x in (("first", first), ("second", second)):
    print(name, "site:", len(x), "windows, mean", round(x.mean(), 1), "spread", round(x.std(ddof=1), 1))

a, b = blocks(first), blocks(second)
diff = b.mean() - a.mean()
both = np.r_[a, b]
null = [both[list(c)].mean() - np.delete(both, list(c)).mean()
        for c in combinations(range(len(both)), len(b))]
print("difference", round(diff, 1), "years, two-sided p", round(np.mean(np.abs(null) >= abs(diff)), 3))

Analyses

For each wearer, privately: the spread of the window estimates, how much consecutive windows agree, and how many independent windows that leaves. Then the two sites: their difference, tested by permuting blocks of five windows, and whether it is shown to lie inside a margin, by an equivalence test of two one-sided tests (Lakens, 2017). Then the room, from the slips: the head's bias and scatter, against the room's median age.

import re
from itertools import combinations
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def estimates(path):
    """Each window's estimate from a saved run of age.py, in order, flagged windows left out."""
    found = (re.match(r"window \d+: (\d+) years", line) for line in open(path)
             if "kept out" not in line)
    return np.array([float(m.group(1)) for m in found if m])

def describe(x):
    """Mean, spread, lag-one autocorrelation, and the windows' worth of independent evidence."""
    r = np.corrcoef(x[:-1], x[1:])[0, 1]
    n_eff = len(x) * (1 - r) / (1 + r)
    return x.mean(), x.std(ddof=1), r, n_eff, x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_eff)

def blocks(x, size=5):
    n = len(x) // size
    return x[:n * size].reshape(n, size).mean(axis=1)

site1, site2 = estimates("w1-site1.txt"), estimates("w1-site2.txt")   # the runs you saved
for name, x in (("site 1", site1), ("site 2", site2)):
    m, sd, r, n_eff, se = describe(x)
    print(f"{name}: {len(x)} windows, mean {m:.1f}, sd {sd:.1f}, lag-one r {r:.2f}, "
          f"worth {n_eff:.0f} independent windows, standard error {se:.1f}")

# The difference between sites, by permuting blocks of five windows.
a, b = blocks(site1), blocks(site2)
diff = b.mean() - a.mean()
both = np.r_[a, b]
null = np.array([both[list(c)].mean() - np.delete(both, list(c)).mean()
                 for c in combinations(range(len(both)), len(b))])
print(f"difference {diff:+.1f} years, two-sided p {np.mean(np.abs(null) >= abs(diff)):.3f}")

# Equivalence: two one-sided tests on the blocks, that the difference is inside 3 years.
margin = 3.0
p_low = stats.ttest_ind(b + margin, a, alternative="greater").pvalue
p_high = stats.ttest_ind(b - margin, a, alternative="less").pvalue
print(f"inside ±{margin:.0f} years: p = {max(p_low, p_high):.3f}")

# The room, from the slips: each wearer's estimate minus their age, and the room's median age
# minus their age, written side by side with no name.
head = np.array([7, 12, 3, 9, 15, 6, 10, -2, 8, 11])
median = np.array([2, -1, 0, 3, 1, -3, 0, -5, 1, 2])
print(f"the head: bias {head.mean():+.1f} years, scatter {head.std(ddof=1):.1f}, "
      f"mean error {np.abs(head).mean():.1f}")
print(f"the room's median age: mean error {np.abs(median).mean():.1f}, "
      f"paired p {stats.wilcoxon(np.abs(head), np.abs(median)).pvalue:.3f}")

Open problems

  1. Any site, tested. A claim that a head reads any site needs the same people scored at several sites, on studies no part of the model saw, compared site by site against an equivalence margin set in advance. The published evaluation scored nine montages and reports their mean, so that comparison is still to be made.
  2. What age is in the EEG. The aperiodic exponent, alpha's frequency and its power all change with age, and how much of a head's skill is any one of them is not known.
  3. The gap as a marker. Whether the gap between predicted and actual age says anything about a person's health, beyond the model's own bias, is debated (Butler and others, 2021).

Report

  1. The preregistration as written, and every deviation from it, each with its reason.
  2. Methods: the sites, the runs, the windows left out and why, the tests and the equivalence margin.
  3. Results: the site comparison wearer by wearer and for the class, then the room's bias and scatter against its median.
  4. What the published evaluation does and doesn't support, and what a test of "any site" would need.
  5. The code, and no one's age or estimate beyond what they put in the box.
