Lab 1 · High school

Alpha Flower

When you close your eyes, how much of your EEG becomes alpha? And what else, from a blink to where the device sits, changes that number?

Background

Read it the night before.

Alpha

EEG records the summed activity of large groups of neurons firing in step, through the skull and scalp, in millionths of a volt. Its rhythms are described by their frequency and amplitude.

Alpha is the rhythm from about 8 to 13 hertz. It is generated in the visual areas at the back of the brain, is strongest over the back of the head with the eyes closed, and is blocked when the eyes open. Hans Berger first recorded it in 1924, in the first human EEG, and Adrian and Matthews traced it to the occipital lobes ten years later.

Alpha's share

Taken apart by frequency, the signal's spectrum shows a peak in the alpha band when the eyes are closed. The number the demo shows is alpha's share: the power from 8 to 13 hertz, as a percentage of all the power from 4 to 30 hertz.

Blinks and muscles make signals too, much bigger than the brain's. Such a signal, one that isn't from the brain, is an artifact, and artifacts can move the number.

Where it goes, and why

Electrode positions on the scalp have standard names, from the 10–20 system. It places each position by fractions of the distances between landmarks: the nasion, between the eyes, the inion, the bump where the skull meets the neck, and the points just in front of the ears. So the same name sits over about the same part of the brain on every head.

Alpha is largest over the occipital lobes, at the back, so the IntoMind One goes on the midline just above the inion, over Oz. Toward the front, alpha fades: the forehead, at Fp1 and Fp2, shows the least alpha and the largest blinks. You will test that in step 5.

Lab 1 · College

Alpha Flower

How much does closing the eyes raise alpha's share of the EEG at the back of the head, against the spread from block to block, and how much of the difference is left at the forehead?

Background

Read it before the lab.

Alpha

Alpha is a rhythm of about 8 to 13 hertz, largest over the back of the head in a person awake with the eyes closed. Berger reported it in 1929. Open the eyes, or turn attention to a task, and it shrinks: alpha blocking.

It comes from the visual and parietal cortex, synchronized through loops with the thalamus. Its frequency and size differ from person to person, so the comparison that means something is within one person: eyes open against eyes closed.

Alpha's share

The demo, its Python program and your code all report the same quantity, for each channel and then averaged over the four:

share = ∫813 S(f) df ÷ ∫430 S(f) df

where S is the power spectral density. They estimate S differently. Four times a second, the demo takes the last three seconds and fits one Hann-windowed segment of 1,024 samples into them, so its number is one periodogram of the two seconds ending about a second ago. Once a second, the program applies Welch's method to the last four seconds, in two-second segments.

The site

In the 10–20 system, Oz sits on the midline a tenth of the nasion-to-inion distance above the inion, over the visual cortex. Fpz is the same distance above the nasion, on the forehead. Alpha's field weakens with distance from its sources, and at Fpz the eyes are close, so blinks are large.

A test with six blocks

Each wearer gives three open and three closed blocks at Oz. If closing the eyes made no difference, every way of choosing which three of the six were closed would be equally likely to give the largest shares. There are C(6, 3) = 20 such ways, so all three closed blocks above all three open ones has a one-sided p of 1/20 = 0.05, the smallest this design can give. Across the class's wearers, a sign test asks how often the closed mean beats the open mean.

Analysis

Load each recording, cut it into its 30-second blocks, leave out the first three seconds of each while the eyes and the rhythm settle, and compute alpha's share per block. Then test each wearer's difference at Oz.

import numpy as np
import mne
from itertools import combinations
from scipy.signal import welch

def share(x, fs, seconds=2):
    """Alpha's share of 4 to 30 Hz power, per channel, averaged over channels."""
    f, p = welch(x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True), fs=fs, nperseg=int(seconds * fs))
    def band(lo, hi):
        pick = (f >= lo) & (f < hi)
        return np.trapezoid(p[:, pick], f[pick], axis=1)
    return float(np.mean(band(8, 13) / band(4, 30)))

def blocks(path, n, skip=3):
    """Alpha's share in each 30-second block of a recording."""
    raw = mne.io.read_raw_bdf(path, preload=True, verbose="error")
    fs = raw.info["sfreq"]
    x = raw.get_data(picks="eeg")
    return [share(x[:, int((30 * b + skip) * fs):int(30 * (b + 1) * fs)], fs) for b in range(n)]

oz = blocks("w1-oz.bdf", 6)            # the path the program printed: open, closed, ...
fpz = blocks("w1-fpz.bdf", 2)          # open, closed
opened, closed = oz[0::2], oz[1::2]
diff = np.mean(closed) - np.mean(opened)
null = [np.mean([oz[i] for i in c]) - np.mean([oz[i] for i in range(6) if i not in c])
        for c in combinations(range(6), 3)]
print("Oz open", np.round(opened, 3), "closed", np.round(closed, 3))
print("difference", round(diff, 3), "one-sided p", np.mean(np.array(null) >= diff))
print("Fpz open", round(fpz[0], 3), "closed", round(fpz[1], 3))

Analyses

For each recording: the spectrum of every block from its fourth second on, the background fitted to the eyes-closed spectrum, and alpha as the periodic part above it. The peak and the center of gravity of that part from 7 to 14 hertz are two estimates of the individual alpha frequency. Reactivity is the log ratio of closed to open power, in a band 2 hertz either side of the center of gravity and in the fixed band. The share is the demo's measure, from the same spectrum.

import numpy as np
import mne
from scipy.signal import welch

BLOCK, SKIP = 45, 3        # seconds in a block, and seconds left out at its start

def spectra(path):
    """Each block's spectrum, averaged over channels: Welch, 4-s Hann segments, half overlap."""
    raw = mne.io.read_raw_bdf(path, preload=True, verbose="error")
    fs = raw.info["sfreq"]
    x = 1e6 * raw.get_data(picks="eeg")                  # microvolts
    blocks = []
    for b in range(int(x.shape[1] / fs // BLOCK)):
        seg = x[:, int((BLOCK * b + SKIP) * fs):int(BLOCK * (b + 1) * fs)]
        f, p = welch(seg, fs=fs, nperseg=int(4 * fs))
        blocks.append(p.mean(axis=0))
    return f, np.array(blocks)                           # open, closed, open, closed

def background(f, p):
    """A line in log-log through 2 to 6 and 15 to 40 Hz: the exponent, and the background."""
    fit = ((f >= 2) & (f < 6)) | ((f >= 15) & (f < 40))
    slope, offset = np.polyfit(np.log10(f[fit]), np.log10(p[fit]), 1)
    return -slope, 10 ** offset * np.maximum(f, 0.25) ** slope

def band(f, p, lo, hi):
    pick = (f >= lo) & (f < hi)
    return np.trapezoid(p[pick], f[pick])

def describe(path):
    f, p = spectra(path)
    opened, closed = p[0::2].mean(axis=0), p[1::2].mean(axis=0)
    exponent, bg = background(f, closed)
    above = np.clip(closed - bg, 0, None)                # the periodic part, eyes closed
    win = (f >= 7) & (f <= 14)
    peak = f[win][np.argmax(above[win])]
    cog = np.sum(f[win] * above[win]) / np.sum(above[win])
    own = band(f, closed, cog - 2, cog + 2) / band(f, opened, cog - 2, cog + 2)
    return dict(peak=peak, cog=cog, exponent=exponent, reactivity=np.log10(own),
                fixed=np.log10(band(f, closed, 8, 13) / band(f, opened, 8, 13)),
                above=band(f, above, cog - 2, cog + 2),
                share=band(f, closed, 8, 13) / band(f, closed, 4, 30))

rows = {f"{w}-{run}": describe(f"{w}-{run}.bdf")       # the paths the program printed
        for w in ("w1", "w2", "w3") for run in ("oz-a", "oz-b", "fpz")}
print(" " * 8 + "".join(f"{k:>11}" for k in rows["w1-oz-a"]))
for name, d in rows.items():
    print(f"{name:8}" + "".join(f"{v:11.2f}" for v in d.values()))

import numpy as np

def icc(a, b):
    """One-way intraclass correlation, from two measurements of each person."""
    x = np.column_stack([a, b])
    between = 2 * np.var(x.mean(axis=1), ddof=1)
    within = np.sum((x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)) ** 2) / len(x)
    return (between - within) / (between + within)

# every wearer in the class: run a, then run b
print(icc([8.86, 10.16, 9.89], [8.91, 10.11, 9.90]))

Open problems

  1. Trait or state. Individual alpha frequency stays put over months of cognitive training (Grandy and others, 2013), yet within a person it rises with cognitive load, by enough that one study found a within-person standard deviation of 0.9 hertz against 2.8 between people (Haegens and others, 2014). How much of a difference between two people is a stable trait, and how much rest pins it down, is open.
  2. What alpha does. On one account, alpha inhibits regions not needed for the task at hand (Jensen and Mazaheri, 2010). The evidence is mostly correlational, and how a rhythm would do that is unsettled.
  3. What opening the eyes changes. Opening the eyes brings in light and raises arousal at once, and both lower alpha (Barry and others, 2007). How much of reactivity is visual input and how much arousal is not settled.

Report

  1. The preregistration as written, and every deviation from it, each with its reason.
  2. Methods to the COBIDAS MEEG recommendations (Pernet and others, 2020): the device, the sites and the reference, the filters in force, the blocks and their timing, the estimator and its parameters, and the exclusions.
  3. Results with the within-person spread before any comparison between people, and the class's intraclass correlations.
  4. What the demo's share would have said about each wearer, and why it differs.
  5. The code, which another group can run on your files to the same numbers.

What happened

Your brain is made of billions of cells called neurons. They signal to each other with tiny pulses of electricity. When millions of them pulse together, in step, the signal is strong enough to pick up through the skull and skin, though it is only millionths of a volt. A recording of it is called EEG.

When you close your eyes, the part of your brain that handles seeing, at the back, has less to do, and many of its neurons fall into step: about ten waves every second. That rhythm is alpha, and it is why the IntoMind One goes on the back of your head. Open your eyes, and alpha fades.

Blinks, talking and moving make electrical signals too, much bigger than the brain's. That is why the skeptic watches for them.
