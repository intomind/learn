Chapter 7 · Middle school

Microcontrollers and circuits

What is the small computer that will switch a light for your brain, and how do you make it blink?

A computer on a chip

A microcontroller is a whole small computer on one chip, built to run one program. There is one inside a remote control, a washing machine, a toy and a car.

The Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W is a small board with a microcontroller, a light, Bluetooth, and two rows of pins. A program can switch a pin on, to 3.3 volts, or off.

A circuit for an LED

An LED is a small light that lights when current flows through it, one way only. Connect one from a pin back to ground, and it lights when the pin is on.

It also needs a resistor, a part that limits how much current flows. Without one, too much current flows, and the LED or the board can be damaged.

Try it

A circuit on paper, then for real

On paper, draw the circuit: a pin, a resistor, an LED, and back to ground. Mark which way the current flows, and which leg of the LED is longer.

Then build it on the breadboard, with the board unplugged: from pin 20, through the resistor and the LED in a row, to ground on pin 18, with the LED's long leg toward pin 20. Have it checked before you plug anything in.

Make it blink

Try it

Blink

Plug the board into the computer with the USB cable, and open the MicroPython editor. Type this program, and run it.

from machine import Pin
import time

led = Pin("LED", Pin.OUT)
while True:
    led.toggle()
    time.sleep(0.5)

Change the 0.5 to 0.1, and run it again. Then change Pin("LED", Pin.OUT) to Pin(15, Pin.OUT), and run it once more, to blink the LED on your breadboard.

You should seeThe board's own light blinking, faster when the number is smaller. Then the LED on your breadboard blinking the same way.

The board can listen

The board can also listen. In the Mind-Controlled Microcontroller, your IntoMind One sends it a score over Bluetooth every few seconds, and the board switches its light from that.

The first time they connect, they pair: they set up secret keys, so anyone who only listens in can't read what passes between them.

  3. In the blink program, what does changing 0.5 to 0.1 do, and why?

Chapter 8 · High school

Microcontrollers and circuits

How does a small board drive an LED safely, run a program, and talk to an IntoMind One over an encrypted link?

A computer on a chip

A microcontroller is a whole small computer on one chip, built to run one program. The Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W is a board with one, a light, a Bluetooth radio, and two rows of pins. Its programs here are written in MicroPython, which is Python made small enough for a microcontroller.

A pin set as an output sits at 3.3 volts or at 0. That on and off is a signal another circuit can use: here, to light an LED.

A pin, and Ohm's law

An LED needs about 2 volts across it, and a small current, a few milliamps. The rest of the pin's 3.3 volts has to drop across a resistor in series with it.

Ohm's law, V = I × R, gives its size: (3.3 − 2.0) volts ÷ 0.004 amps is about 330 ohms. Without a resistor, the current is limited only by the pin and the LED, and one of them can be damaged.

Bluetooth Low Energy

A Bluetooth Low Energy device advertises: it broadcasts a short packet a few times a second, saying what it is. A central, such as the board or your computer, scans, connects, and pairs: it sets up keys, so the link is encrypted. Bonding keeps those keys for next time.

Then the central reads and writes the device's values, and subscribes to notifications: values the device sends whenever they change. The board will subscribe to your custom AI head's score.

        print(found.name, found.rssi)

asyncio.run(main())

Chapter 9 · College

Microcontrollers and circuits

How does a small board drive a pin safely, find and pair with an IntoMind One over Bluetooth Low Energy, and how long does a change of mind take to reach that pin?

A pin, and Ohm's law

A microcontroller is a small computer on one chip, built to run one program. The Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W carries one, a radio for Bluetooth, and forty pins. A pin set as an output sits at 3.3 volts or at 0, and can source a few milliamps: enough for an LED, or a signal into another board's input with the grounds joined.

An LED drops about 2 volts. The rest falls across a resistor in series, sized by Ohm's law, V = IR: (3.3 − 2.0) V ÷ 4 mA ≈ 330 Ω. Nothing on the pin ever connects to anything that plugs into a wall.

Bluetooth Low Energy

A Bluetooth Low Energy peripheral advertises, a central scans and connects, and the peripheral exposes services, each a set of characteristics with properties: read, write, notify, indicate. A notification arrives unacknowledged, and an indication is acknowledged, so it isn't lost.

The IntoMind One advertises one service. A client writes commands to one characteristic, receives each answer by indication on another, and subscribes to notifications for the EEG, the predictions and the status. It accepts commands and sends data only over an encrypted, bonded link.

Try it

A connection, traced

    print("firmware", info.firmware, "channels", info.channels)

    print("selected", active)

    await device.disconnect()

asyncio.run(main())

You should seeThe firmware version, four channels, the sample rates and the number of head slots, then each slot with its state, its number of outputs and its name.

Pairing, and what it protects

Pairing sets up the keys that encrypt the link, and bonding keeps them for the next connection. With LE Secure Connections, every pairing method uses an elliptic curve key exchange that defeats a passive eavesdropper, even one who heard the pairing.

The methods differ in what else they defend. Just Works asks nothing of the user, so it can't detect an active attacker in the middle of the pairing, and it accepts any device in range that asks to pair with an IntoMind One not already connected. Numeric comparison and passkey entry need a display or a keypad, and out-of-band pairing a second channel, such as NFC. The IntoMind One pairs with Just Works.

Closed loops, and latency

A closed loop measures, decides and acts. Its latency is the sum of its stages: the four-second window, the time for a window to fill with the new state, the model's time on the chip, the link, and the program. The window dominates. Shortening it gives the model less signal, and costs accuracy.

Chapter 9 · Graduate

Microcontrollers and circuits

What does closing the loop on a small board add: in delay, in ways to fail, and in ways to be attacked?

The program on the board

The Mind-Controlled Microcontroller runs one MicroPython program on a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W. It scans for three seconds, keeps the IntoMind Ones whose names start with a prefix, empty by default, and connects to the one heard most strongly. It selects the head in slot 2, starts the stream unless one is running, and turns on predictions. Each clean reading sets the decision, On when the score is above 0.5. A reading with a gap in the signal changes nothing, and the LED blinks fast to say so.

Its fail-safes: the decision is Off at start and whenever the link is lost, and the link counts as lost after 20 seconds without a reading. It retries every 5 seconds, and after two connections in a row that fail, it forgets its pairings and pairs again. A pin set as an output switches between 0 and 3.3 volts, which drives an LED through a resistor, (3.3 − 2.0) volts over 4 milliamps or about 330 ohms, or a signal for anything that runs on the same low voltage.

Delay, as a distribution

The device describes one four-second window every four seconds, on a grid that starts with the stream. A change of state falls somewhere inside a window, and if a window's score follows the share of it spent in the new state, the first window more than half new comes 2 to 6 seconds after the change, uniformly, before the model, the link and the program add their own. Shorter windows would answer sooner with less signal each. The closed loop's delay is a distribution, and its tail matters as much as its middle.

False switches, per hour

At one window every four seconds a loop makes 900 decisions an hour. If each Off window crosses the threshold with probability α, independently, the pin goes high about 900α times an hour. People who might rely on a brain-computer interface ask for accidental activations no more than once every two to four hours (Huggins, Wren and Gruis, 2011), so α near 1 in 2,000. A higher threshold or a run of k windows in a row, roughly αk, buys that with delay. The board's program uses neither, which suits a demonstration and wouldn't suit someone's front door.

Pairing, and a threat model

The IntoMind One requires an encrypted, bonded link for everything but its device information, paired by LE Secure Connections with Just Works, since it has no display or keypad. The elliptic-curve exchange defeats a passive eavesdropper, but a pairing that asks for no code can't tell whether an active attacker sits between the two at that moment. Bonding keeps the keys for later connections, for up to four computers.

A threat model names the assets, the adversaries and the surfaces (Denning, Matsuoka and Kohno, 2009). Here the assets are the pin's state, the head, and what flows over the link. An adversary in radio range can pair with an IntoMind One that isn't connected, since anyone can, and any connected program can install a head, since the device checks a head's shape and hash and never who made it. The board connects to the strongest device whose name fits its prefix, so a stronger impostor with that name is chosen first. Jamming the link only forces the fail-safe, Off. Signals from the brain can leak more than their purpose (Martinovic and others, 2012), and attacks on brain-computer interfaces raise their own ethical questions (Ienca and Haselager, 2016).

Analysis

Analysis

Delay and false switches, traded

Simulate the loop's delay, with windows on a fixed grid and a change of state at a random moment, and its false switches over hundreds of hours at rest, for two thresholds and for one or two windows in a row. Off scores sit near 0.2 and On near 0.8, with a spread of 0.15 from window to window.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
W = 4.0           # seconds in a window, and between windows
DELTA = 0.5       # an assumed delay for the model, the link and the program: measure yours

def runs(above, hold):
    """True where `hold` scores in a row have been above the threshold."""
    count = np.zeros(above.shape, int)
    for j in range(above.shape[1]):
        prev = count[:, j - 1] if j else 0
        count[:, j] = np.where(above[:, j], prev + 1, 0)
    return count >= hold

def latency(threshold, hold, off=0.2, on=0.8, noise=0.15, trials=20000):
    """Seconds from a change to On until the pin, with windows on a fixed grid. A window's
    score moves from Off's toward On's with the share of it spent in the new state."""
    t0 = rng.uniform(0, W, trials)                 # where the change falls in its window
    ends = W * np.arange(1, 9)
    share = np.clip((ends[None] - t0[:, None]) / W, 0, 1)
    score = off + share * (on - off) + rng.normal(0, noise, share.shape)
    first = np.argmax(runs(score > threshold, hold), axis=1)
    return ends[first] - t0 + DELTA

def false_switches_per_hour(threshold, hold, off=0.2, noise=0.15, hours=500):
    """How often the pin goes high while the wearer stays Off."""
    on = runs((off + rng.normal(0, noise, (1, int(hours * 3600 / W)))) > threshold, hold)[0]
    return np.sum(on[1:] & ~on[:-1]) / hours

for threshold, hold in ((0.5, 1), (0.5, 2), (0.6, 1), (0.6, 2)):
    lat = latency(threshold, hold)
    print(f"threshold {threshold}, {hold} in a row: median delay {np.median(lat):.1f} s, "
          f"90 percent within {np.percentile(lat, 90):.1f} s, "
          f"{false_switches_per_hour(threshold, hold):.2f} false switches an hour")

You should seeAt the program's rule, a threshold of 0.5 and one window, a median delay of 4.5 seconds, 90 percent within 6.6, and about 20 false switches an hour. Two windows in a row cut that to about one every two hours, and double the delay. A threshold of 0.6 with two in a row gives about one false switch in a hundred hours, with 90 percent of switches within 15 seconds.

Open problems

  1. Authenticating without a screen. Pairing that resists an active attacker needs a display, a keypad or a second channel, which small wearables and boards lack. Doing it without burdening the user is open.
  2. Safe defaults for uncertain decisions. A decoder's output is uncertain every time, and how a system should act on it, when to hold, when to ask, and when to do nothing, depends on what acting costs. General rules don't exist.
  3. Who may install what. Heads are weights, never code, and still change what a device does. How to let people install their own while keeping out heads nobody asked for is unsettled.

Problems

  1. Derive the delay from a change of state to the first window that is more than half new, when the change falls uniformly within a four-second window. What are its mean and its standard deviation?

  2. Off windows cross the threshold with probability 0.02, independently. How many false switches an hour at one window, and at two in a row? What does the independence assumption hide?

  3. Why is Off the right state for the pin when the link is lost? When might it not be?

  4. An attacker wants the board to switch on. List two ways the setup allows, and a mitigation for each.
