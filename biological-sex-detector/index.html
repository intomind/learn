Lab 4 · College

Biological Sex Detector

An AI model on the IntoMind One scores EEG between two groups that overlap far more than they differ. What can a score this weak say about one person, and what must it never be used to say?

Background

Read it before the lab.

What the head was taught

The sex head was fitted on 194,940 four-second windows from 3,986 people in 95 studies, each person labeled 0 for male and 1 for female, as the studies recorded them. Its output is a least-squares score on that scale, not a probability, and it can fall below 0 or above 1.

The labels are the category each study recorded, however it decided it. The evaluation doesn't say whether the studies asked about sex or about gender, and two categories don't describe everyone's biology. The score measures only how much a recording resembles the training data's two groups.

How weak, exactly

Averaged over a person's windows, the head's AUC on studies it never saw is 0.623, and band power from the same channels gets 0.614. On one window, 0.588 against 0.582. If both groups' scores are normal with the same spread, the AUC fixes the separation of their means:

AUC = Φ(d′ / √2), so d′ = √2 Φ⁻¹(AUC)

That is about 0.44 standard deviations: the clouds overlap almost entirely. A call at the midpoint is right with probability Φ(d′/2), and what a call for one group is worth then depends on how common that group is.

Beating chance isn't a difference in kind

Small average differences can beat chance while almost everyone overlaps, and some of them may not be about the brain at all: head size and skull thickness change band power, and so can hair and contact. The published evaluation's own verdict is that the head is not a useful predictor of a person's sex, and is not offered as one. It shows what the embedding carries.

Analysis

From the published AUCs alone, work out each separation, how often a call at the midpoint is right, and what a call for one group is worth as that group becomes rare. Then ask what averaging would give if a person's windows were independent.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

for name, auc in (("one window", 0.588), ("a person's windows", 0.623)):
    d = np.sqrt(2) * norm.ppf(auc)             # the separation of two normal groups, in SDs
    right = norm.cdf(d / 2)                    # a call at the midpoint, with equal groups
    print(f"{name}: d' {d:.2f}, a call right {right:.0%} of the time with equal groups")
    for base in (0.5, 0.1, 0.01):
        ppv = base * right / (base * right + (1 - base) * (1 - right))
        print(f"    one group at {base:.0%} of people: a call for it is right {ppv:.0%}")

# If a person's windows were independent, averaging 40 of them would multiply d' by √40.
d1 = np.sqrt(2) * norm.ppf(0.588)
print("forty independent windows would give an AUC of", round(norm.cdf(d1 * np.sqrt(40) / np.sqrt(2)), 2))

Lab 4 · Graduate

Biological Sex Detector

A score separates two labeled groups' EEG at an AUC near 0.6. What does it measure, how far could it ever go, and what would it take to show that it reads the brain rather than the head, the hair or the contact?

Background

Joel and others (2015) found that brains in MRI rarely have all their features at one sex's end, and called them mosaics. Chekroud and others (2016) answered that a multivariate classifier still separates the groups well, and Del Giudice and others (2016) that the mosaic method would miss large differences. Both can be true: groups can be told apart in many dimensions while individuals mix features. In EEG, a deep network trained on clinical recordings reached more than 80 percent accuracy, with fast beta, 20 to 25 hertz, its main feature (van Putten, Olbrich and Arns, 2018), a band where scalp recordings also carry muscle.

A score can separate groups through anything that differs between them on average and changes the signal: head size, hair under the electrodes, contact, age, the recording site, the study. Skull thickness, often suspected, explained little of alpha power between people in one study (Hagemann and others, 2008), which is the kind of test each suspect needs. Rippon and others (2014) set out principles for such research, from treating sex and gender as variables with many causes to reporting overlap, not only means.

Analyses

From the published AUCs, with two normal groups of equal spread: the separation on one window and averaged, the share of a window's score that is stable within a person, and the AUC that averaging could never exceed. Then simulate a score that reads only contact, where contact differs between the groups, and adjust it for a measured index of contact, such as power from 30 to 45 hertz. Then a score with a real difference and contact that doesn't differ.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm, rankdata

def auc(score, group):
    """The chance that a random member of group 1 outscores a random member of group 0."""
    r = rankdata(score)
    n1 = group.sum()
    return (r[group == 1].sum() - n1 * (n1 + 1) / 2) / (n1 * (len(group) - n1))

# 1. What the published AUCs imply, with two normal groups of equal spread.
d1, dm = (np.sqrt(2) * norm.ppf(a) for a in (0.588, 0.623))
m = 40                                   # the most windows averaged for one person
stable = (1 / (dm / d1) ** 2 - 1 / m) / (1 - 1 / m)
ceiling = d1 / np.sqrt(stable)
print(f"d' {d1:.2f} on one window, {dm:.2f} averaged")
print(f"the share of a window's score that is stable within a person: about {stable:.2f}")
print(f"averaging without limit: d' {ceiling:.2f}, AUC {norm.cdf(ceiling / np.sqrt(2)):.3f}")

# 2. A score that reads contact and nothing of the brain, when contact differs by group.
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n = 4000
group = rng.integers(0, 2, n)                     # the label the studies recorded
hair = rng.normal(0, 1, n) + 1.0 * group          # hair under the electrodes, more in group 1
contact = -0.8 * hair + rng.normal(0, 1, n)       # thicker hair, poorer contact
score = -0.6 * contact + rng.normal(0, 1, n)      # poorer contact, higher score: no brain term
proxy = contact + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)           # a measured index of contact, with error
adjusted = score - np.polyval(np.polyfit(proxy, score, 1), proxy)
print(f"contact only: AUC {auc(score, group):.3f}, "
      f"after adjusting for the index {auc(adjusted, group):.3f}")

# 3. The same, with a real difference and contact that doesn't differ by group.
contact = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
score = 0.3 * group - 0.5 * contact + rng.normal(0, 1, n)
proxy = contact + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)
adjusted = score - np.polyval(np.polyfit(proxy, score, 1), proxy)
print(f"a real difference: AUC {auc(score, group):.3f}, "
      f"after adjusting {auc(adjusted, group):.3f}")

Then report:

  1. Derive the stable share from the two AUCs, and say what it means that averaging stops near 0.62.
  2. From the simulation, what adjusting for contact does to a score that reads contact and to one that doesn't, and what a noisy index would leave behind.
  3. Design the study that would separate brain from contact: what you would measure, in whom, and the comparison that would decide it.

Open problems

  1. Brain, or body? How much of any EEG classifier's separation of the sexes comes from the brain rather than anatomy, hair, contact or muscle is not known, for this head or the deep network that found more.
  2. Mosaics and classifiers. Whether brains are better described as two types or as mosaics depends on the question, and the debate Joel and others (2015) opened continues.
  3. What the labels mean. Most datasets record two categories without saying how they were assigned. What a classifier learns from such labels, and for whom it fails, is open.
