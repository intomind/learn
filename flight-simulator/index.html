What happened

What you see is handled first at the back of your brain, in the visual cortex. When you look at a light that flickers at a steady speed, the neurons there start pulsing at that same speed.

Each light in the demo flickers at its own speed: 8, 10, 12 or 15 times a second. So the speed that shows up in your EEG says which light you are looking at, and the ship turns toward it. The IntoMind One never watches your eyes.

Lab 2 · High school

BCI Flight Simulator

How can a computer tell which of four lights you are looking at, from the back of your brain? And why is one of them harder than the rest?

This demo flickers

Flicker between about 3 and 60 times a second can trigger a seizure in people with photosensitive epilepsy. Don't play it or watch it if you have epilepsy, or if flickering light has ever made you feel unwell.

Whether you play or watch, sit back from the screen, in a well-lit room. If you feel strange while it runs, cover one eye with your hand, turn away from the screen, and tell your teacher.

Background

Read it the night before.

Frequency tagging

A light flickering at a steady rate drives the visual cortex at that rate. The response is a steady-state visually evoked potential, or SSVEP. It is too small to pick out of the EEG by eye, and clear in its spectrum: a peak at the light's rate, and smaller ones at its harmonics.

Give each light its own rate, and the rate that stands out in the EEG says which light has your attention. That is frequency tagging.

The baseline, and the score

The demo's lights flicker at 8, 10, 12 and 15 hertz. Your brain makes rhythms of its own, and alpha sits right around 10. So before the lights start, the demo listens for ten seconds and measures how much of each rate is already there: its baseline.

Then, four times a second, it takes the last two seconds of signal, and scores each rate by its power compared with the power just beside it, against its baseline. A rate that stands out for two updates in a row turns the ship.

Where it goes, and why

The response comes from the primary visual cortex, the first part of the brain to handle what the eyes see, at the back of the head on both sides of the midline. It is strongest on the midline just above the inion, at Oz, weaker one step to either side, at O1 and O2, and weak at the front.

Lab 2 · College

BCI Flight Simulator

Can you find each light's flicker rate in your own EEG, rebuild the demo's score from its definition, and measure what a faster decision costs in accuracy?

This demo flickers

Flicker between about 3 and 60 times a second can trigger a seizure in people with photosensitive epilepsy. Don't play it or watch it if you have epilepsy, or if flickering light has ever made you feel unwell.

Whether you play or watch, sit back from the screen, in a well-lit room. If you feel strange while it runs, cover one eye with your hand, turn away from the screen, and tell your instructor.

Background

Read it before the lab.

Frequency tagging

A light flickering at a steady rate drives the primary visual cortex at that rate, with smaller responses at its harmonics: the steady-state visually evoked potential. Give each choice its own rate, and the rate that stands out in the EEG says which one has the eyes. The response is largest over the occipital pole, at Oz, and small beside the EEG's own rhythms, so it is found in the spectrum, not the trace.

The demo's score

Four times a second, on the last two seconds of the four channels, the demo computes for each light's rate f:

score = r(f) + ½ max(0, r(2f) − 1)

where r(f) is the power at exactly f, from a Hann-windowed segment and averaged over the channels, divided by the mean power at 1, 1.5 and 2 hertz either side. A score near 1 means nothing is there.

Each score is divided by its average over a ten-second baseline taken with the lights steady, or by 1 if that is smaller. A light counts when its score reaches 2.5 and is 1.25 times the next, two updates in a row, and lets go after two updates without.

Speed against accuracy

A longer window resolves frequency more finely and averages away more noise, but every decision waits for it. Wolpaw's information transfer rate puts both in one number. With N choices, each right with probability P, a decision carries

B = log2 N + P log2 P + (1 − P) log2((1 − P) / (N − 1))

bits, and a decision every T seconds delivers B × 60 / T bits a minute.

Analysis

Plot each recording's spectrum, with 8, 10, 12 and 15 hertz and their harmonics marked. Then compute the demo's score yourself, on windows of 1, 2 and 4 seconds a quarter second apart, divide each light's score by its mean with the eyes on the center, and count how often the looked-at light scores highest.

import numpy as np
import mne
from scipy.signal.windows import hann

RATES = {"top": 8, "right": 10, "bottom": 12, "left": 15}
NEAR = (-2, -1.5, -1, 1, 1.5, 2)

def power(x, fs, hz):
    """Power at exactly hz, Hann-windowed, averaged over channels, as the demo does."""
    n = x.shape[1]
    xs = (x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)) * hann(n, sym=False)
    k = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * hz * np.arange(n) / fs)
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(xs @ k) ** 2)) / n ** 2

def score(x, fs, hz):
    snr = lambda f: power(x, fs, f) / np.mean([power(x, fs, f + d) for d in NEAR])
    return snr(hz) + 0.5 * max(0.0, snr(2 * hz) - 1)

def windows(path, seconds, step=0.25):
    """Every light's score in each window of a recording, a quarter second apart."""
    raw = mne.io.read_raw_bdf(path, preload=True, verbose="error")
    fs = raw.info["sfreq"]
    x = raw.get_data(picks="eeg")
    n = int(seconds * fs)
    return [{d: score(x[:, i:i + n], fs, hz) for d, hz in RATES.items()}
            for i in range(0, x.shape[1] - n + 1, int(step * fs))]

def bits_a_minute(n, p, seconds):
    """Wolpaw's information transfer rate, for n choices each right with probability p."""
    if p <= 1 / n:
        return 0.0
    bits = np.log2(n) + p * np.log2(p)
    if p < 1:
        bits += (1 - p) * np.log2((1 - p) / (n - 1))
    return bits * 60 / seconds

for seconds in (1, 2, 4):
    # each light's score with the eyes on the center: the demo's baseline, divided out
    center = windows("center.bdf", seconds)
    base = {d: max(1.0, np.mean([s[d] for s in center])) for d in RATES}
    right = total = 0
    for d, hz in RATES.items():
        for s in windows(f"look-{hz}.bdf", seconds):
            adjusted = {k: v / base[k] for k, v in s.items()}
            right += max(adjusted, key=adjusted.get) == d
            total += 1
    p = right / total
    print(seconds, "s:", round(p, 2), "right,", round(bits_a_minute(4, p, seconds), 1), "bits a minute")

Analyses

For one pilot: the demo's detector rebuilt and run on each look, update by update. Then forced choices on windows of 0.5 to 4 seconds, by the demo's score and by canonical correlation with sinusoids at each rate and its harmonics (Lin and others, 2006), with Wolpaw's bits a minute and half a second added to each choice for moving the eyes. Then each rate's signal-to-noise ratio at O1 and at O2, with the eyes on the center. Change the w1 in the labels for each pilot.

import numpy as np
import mne
from scipy.signal import butter, sosfiltfilt
from scipy.signal.windows import hann

RATES = {"top": 8, "right": 10, "bottom": 12, "left": 15}
NEAR = (-2, -1.5, -1, 1, 1.5, 2)

def load(path):
    raw = mne.io.read_raw_bdf(path, preload=True, verbose="error")
    return raw.info["sfreq"], 1e6 * raw.get_data(picks="eeg")

def power(x, fs, hz):
    """Power at exactly hz, Hann-windowed, averaged over the channels, as the demo does."""
    n = x.shape[1]
    xs = (x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)) * hann(n, sym=False)
    k = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * hz * np.arange(n) / fs)
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(xs @ k) ** 2)) / n ** 2

def snr(x, fs, hz):
    return power(x, fs, hz) / np.mean([power(x, fs, hz + d) for d in NEAR])

def score(x, fs, hz):
    return snr(x, fs, hz) + 0.5 * max(0.0, snr(x, fs, 2 * hz) - 1)

def cca(x, fs, hz, harmonics=3):
    """The largest canonical correlation of the channels with sinusoids at hz's harmonics."""
    t = np.arange(x.shape[1]) / fs
    y = np.array([f(2 * np.pi * k * hz * t) for k in range(1, harmonics + 1)
                  for f in (np.sin, np.cos)])
    qx = np.linalg.qr((x - x.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)).T)[0]
    qy = np.linalg.qr((y - y.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)).T)[0]
    return np.linalg.svd(qx.T @ qy, compute_uv=False)[0]

def windows(x, fs, seconds, step=0.25):
    n = int(seconds * fs)
    return [x[:, i:i + n] for i in range(0, x.shape[1] - n + 1, int(step * fs))]

def held(adjusted, threshold=2.5, margin=1.25, hold=2, release=2):
    """The demo's detector, update by update: the light it holds after each, or None."""
    current = pending = None
    count = empty = 0
    out = []
    for s in adjusted:
        best, second = sorted(s.values())[::-1][:2]
        top = max(s, key=s.get) if best >= threshold and best >= margin * second else None
        if top is None:
            pending, count, empty = None, 0, empty + 1
            current = None if empty >= release else current
        else:
            empty = 0
            if top == current:
                pending, count = None, 0
            elif top == pending:
                count += 1
                if count >= hold:
                    current, pending, count = top, None, 0
            else:
                pending, count = top, 1
        out.append(current)
    return out

def bits_a_minute(p, seconds, n=4):
    """Wolpaw's information transfer rate: n choices, each right with probability p."""
    if p <= 1 / n:
        return 0.0
    bits = np.log2(n) + p * np.log2(p)
    if p < 1:
        bits += (1 - p) * np.log2((1 - p) / (n - 1))
    return bits * 60 / seconds

looks = {d: load(f"{PILOT}-look-{hz}.bdf")[1] for d, hz in RATES.items()}

base = {d: max(1.0, np.mean([score(w, fs, hz) for w in windows(steady, fs, 2)]))
        for d, hz in RATES.items()}
for d, x in looks.items():
    h = held([{k: score(w, fs, hz) / base[k] for k, hz in RATES.items()}
              for w in windows(x, fs, 2)])
    right, nothing = np.mean([v == d for v in h]), np.mean([v is None for v in h])
    print(f"looking {d:6s} held: right {right:.2f}, wrong {1 - right - nothing:.2f}, "
          f"nothing {nothing:.2f}")

# 2. Forced choices on windows of each length: the demo's score against canonical correlation,
#    which works on the signal band-passed from 6 to 50 Hz, as Lin and others (2006) did.
GAZE = 0.5                          # seconds to move the eyes, added to each choice
bp = butter(4, [6, 50], "bandpass", fs=fs, output="sos")
passed = {d: sosfiltfilt(bp, x, axis=1) for d, x in looks.items()}
for seconds in (0.5, 1, 2, 4):
    base = {d: max(1.0, np.mean([score(w, fs, hz) for w in windows(steady, fs, seconds)]))
            for d, hz in RATES.items()}
    right, n = {"score": 0, "cca": 0}, 0
    for d, x in looks.items():
        for w, v in zip(windows(x, fs, seconds), windows(passed[d], fs, seconds)):
            s = {k: score(w, fs, hz) / base[k] for k, hz in RATES.items()}
            c = {k: cca(v, fs, hz) for k, hz in RATES.items()}
            right["score"] += max(s, key=s.get) == d
            right["cca"] += max(c, key=c.get) == d
            n += 1
    print(f"{seconds} s: " + "   ".join(
        f"{k} {v / n:.2f} right, {bits_a_minute(v / n, seconds + GAZE):.0f} bits a minute"
        for k, v in right.items()))

# 3. Eyes on the center, lights flickering: each rate's response at O1 and at O2.
for site in ("o1", "o2"):
    fs, x = load(f"{PILOT}-center-{site}.bdf")
    print(site, "  ".join(f"{d} {np.mean([snr(w, fs, hz) for w in windows(x, fs, 4, 1)]):.2f}"
                          for d, hz in RATES.items()))

Open problems

  1. Without the eyes. The flight steers by gaze, which a person who can't move their eyes doesn't have. With covert attention alone, 6 of 11 people reached 75 percent correct in at least one session (Kelly and others, 2005). Making attention without gaze reliable is open.
  2. Comfort against signal. Flicker from about 3 to 60 hertz is visible, tiring and carries a risk of seizures (Fisher and others, 2005). Faster or fainter flicker is gentler and gives a weaker response, and how far that can be pushed is unsettled.
  3. Calibration. Decoders calibrated to each person do far better than the demo's: with forty targets, 0.5-second flickers and a calibrated decoder, Chen and others (2015) reached 5.32 bits a second, and task-related component analysis averaged 325 bits a minute when cued and 199 when spelling freely (Nakanishi and others, 2018). Keeping that across days and people without recalibrating is open.
  4. Reporting speed. Information transfer rates are often computed in ways that break their assumptions (Yuan and others, 2013), and cued rates overstate free use, as the gap between 325 and 199 shows.

Report

  1. The preregistration as written, and every deviation from it, each with its reason.
  2. Methods to the COBIDAS MEEG recommendations: the device, the sites, the filters in force, the screen and its distance, the recordings, both decoders and every parameter.
  3. Results for each pilot first, then the class's, with how the overlap of windows was handled.
  4. The half-field result, and what it does and doesn't say about where the responses come from.
  5. The code, which another group can run on your files to the same numbers.
