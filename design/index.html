Engineers build in a loop called the engineering design process: define the problem, imagine ideas, plan one, create it, test it, improve it, and test again.

Choosing two states

Choose two states you can each hold for a minute, that don't need blinking or moving your jaw, and that are likely to look different in your EEG. Resting with your eyes open, against counting down in your head, works for many people.

Blinks and a clenched jaw make signals far bigger than your brain's. A project built on them would be reading your muscles, not your mind.

Designing a BCI

Who would your brain-computer interface help, and how will you know it works for them? Turn a need into requirements, and write the test before you build.

Who brain-computer interfaces are for

Brain-computer interfaces were first developed for people who cannot move or speak, such as people with advanced ALS, a disease that takes away the control of the muscles. For them, an interface that is slow and imperfect can still mean calling for help, answering yes or no, or switching something on without help.

Engineers design in a loop called the engineering design process, and it starts with the person: what they need to do, what stops them, and what would make an interface worth wearing.

Requirements

A requirement turns a need into something a test can check. When 61 people with ALS were asked what a brain-computer interface would have to do, most would be satisfied by one that is right at least 9 times in 10, as fast as 15 to 19 letters a minute, and that leaves its standby by mistake no more than once every 2 to 4 hours.

Each of those is a number a test can check. Yours will be too.

Two kinds of mistakes

A switch can be wrong two ways. A false positive switches to On when nobody meant it. A false negative stays Off when the wearer meant On.

The threshold sets the balance between them. The board compares each score with SWITCH_THRESHOLD, 0.5 unless you change it. Raise it, and there are fewer false positives and more false negatives. Which mistake costs more depends on the person and the purpose.

A test you write first

Write the test before you build, and keep to it. Then nobody, including you, can move the bar after seeing the result. Scientists call this preregistration.

Ask for more than luck. A light that only guessed would follow 8 or more of 10 switches about 5 times in 100.

Chapter 10 · College

Designing a BCI

Who would your brain-computer interface serve, what must it do for them, stated so a test can check it, and where should its threshold sit when the two kinds of mistake cost different amounts?

Who brain-computer interfaces are for

Brain-computer interfaces were developed first for people who can't move or speak, such as people with advanced ALS. A person with locked-in syndrome has used a fully implanted interface to communicate at home, and noninvasive ones have been used for years in research.

User-centered design evaluates an interface with the people it is for, by its effectiveness, its efficiency and their satisfaction, not only by accuracy with healthy volunteers. Reliability, setup time and independence matter to users in ways a lab's accuracy doesn't measure.

Requirements a test can check

A requirement names a metric, a threshold, the conditions and the users. When 61 people with ALS were asked what an interface would have to do, most would be satisfied by one right at least 9 times in 10, as fast as 15 to 19 letters a minute, and leaving its standby by mistake no more than once every 2 to 4 hours.

What users needA requirementHow to test it
EffectivenessThe custom AI head clearly better than chance, in every session, for the user.The permutation test over rounds, in each session.
EfficiencyThe pin following at least 8 of 10 calls, each within 8 seconds.Ten calls at random times, timed.

SatisfactionA usability score above an agreed line.A standard questionnaire, such as the System Usability Scale.

The operating point

The threshold chooses a point on the ROC curve: raise it, and false positives fall while false negatives rise. When the two cost different amounts, choose the threshold that minimizes the expected cost,

cost = cFP · P(false positive) + cFN · P(false negative)

estimated on data the head never trained on. A call for help, where a miss costs far more than a false alarm, wants a low threshold. A switch that does something hard to undo wants a high one.

Chapter 10 · Graduate

Designing a BCI

Given a person and a purpose, how do you specify a brain-computer interface, choose where it operates, and decide whether it is good enough, before you build it and after?

Users first

User-centered design evaluates an interface by its effectiveness, its efficiency and its users' satisfaction, with the people it is for, in their setting (Kübler and others, 2014). Asked what a brain-computer interface would have to do, most of 61 people with ALS would be satisfied with one that was right at least 90 percent of the time, that spelled 15 to 19 letters a minute, and that left its standby by mistake no more than once every 2 to 4 hours (Huggins, Wren and Gruis, 2011). A requirement turns such an answer into a metric, a threshold, the conditions and the users, so that a test can check it.

Metrics that mean something

Wolpaw's information transfer rate (Wolpaw and others, 2002) summarizes speed and accuracy in bits a minute, under assumptions that interfaces rarely meet: choices equally likely, errors spread evenly, and no cost to correcting them (Thompson and others, 2014). A utility metric counts what the user gains, with the time errors cost to undo (Dal Seno, Matteucci and Mainardi, 2010). For a switch, the numbers that matter are the delay to a deliberate switch, its tail as much as its middle, and the false switches per hour of meaning nothing. An interface used asynchronously, whenever the user wants, has a no-control state, and keeping it quiet is often harder than telling two states apart (Mason and Birch, 2000).

The operating point

A threshold trades the two errors. With the share of time spent in On, π, and the costs of a false switch and a missed one, the expected cost of a decision is

cFP (1 − π) P(score > θ | Off) + cFN π P(score ≤ θ | On)

and the threshold that minimizes it is where the scores' likelihood ratio, On over Off, equals cFP(1 − π) / (cFN π). A run of k windows above it lowers false switches, by about the k-th power only if neighboring windows are independent, and adds k − 1 windows of delay. When the cost of acting wrongly is high, the system can also wait, ask, or let the user undo, and the brain's own response to an error can help it notice (Chavarriaga, Sobolewski and Millán, 2014).

Analysis

Analysis

An operating point that meets two requirements

Two requirements: no more than one false switch every two hours, and 90 percent of deliberate switches within 15 seconds. Simulate a head's scores with neighboring windows alike, as real ones are, and search thresholds and holds for one that meets both. Then put in your own head's numbers from the light switch.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)
W = 4.0                       # a score every four seconds
OFF, ON, SD, RHO = 0.2, 0.8, 0.15, 0.5   # your head's scores: means, spread, and how alike
                                         # neighboring windows are. Put in your own.

def noise(rows, cols):
    """Window-to-window noise that carries over from one window to the next."""
    e = rng.normal(0, SD, (rows, cols))
    for j in range(1, cols):
        e[:, j] = RHO * e[:, j - 1] + np.sqrt(1 - RHO ** 2) * e[:, j]
    return e

def held(above, hold):
    """Where `hold` windows in a row have been above the threshold."""
    count = np.zeros(above.shape, int)
    for j in range(above.shape[1]):
        count[:, j] = np.where(above[:, j], (count[:, j - 1] if j else 0) + 1, 0)
    return count >= hold

def false_per_hour(threshold, hold, hours=200):
    on = held(OFF + noise(1, int(hours * 3600 / W)) > threshold, hold)[0]
    return np.sum(on[1:] & ~on[:-1]) / hours

def slow_tail(threshold, hold, trials=5000):
    """The 90th percentile of the delay from a change to On, with windows on a grid."""
    t0 = rng.uniform(0, W, trials)
    ends = W * np.arange(1, 13)
    share = np.clip((ends[None] - t0[:, None]) / W, 0, 1)
    h = held(OFF + share * (ON - OFF) + noise(trials, len(ends)) > threshold, hold)
    return np.percentile(ends[np.argmax(h, axis=1)] - t0, 90)

print("threshold  hold  false an hour  90% of switches within")
for threshold in (0.5, 0.6, 0.7):
    for hold in (1, 2, 3):
        f, tail = false_per_hour(threshold, hold), slow_tail(threshold, hold)
        ok = "  meets both" if f <= 0.5 and tail <= 15 else ""
        print(f"{threshold:9.1f} {hold:5d} {f:14.2f} {tail:13.1f} s{ok}")

You should seeOne rule meeting both, a threshold of 0.7 with no hold: 0.39 false switches an hour, and 90 percent within 12.3 seconds. The demo's rule, 0.5 with no hold, gives 17 false switches an hour. Holds help less than independence would suggest: at 0.5, two in a row still leave 2.8 an hour.

Then report: the rule your own head's scores allow, or which requirement it can't meet, and what would have to change.

Open problems

  1. Metrics that predict use. Which laboratory measures predict whether someone keeps using an interface at home is not settled (Thompson and others, 2014).
  2. Home use. A noninvasive interface was used independently at home by people with ALS for up to 18 months, though 14 of the 42 who enrolled got that far (Wolpaw and others, 2018), and an implanted one by a person with ALS in daily life (Vansteensel and others, 2016). What it takes for more people to keep using noninvasive ones is open.
  3. Meaning nothing. Recognizing that a person doesn't intend anything, reliably and for hours, is the hard part of asynchronous control, and no general solution exists.

Problems

  1. Turn "leaves standby by mistake no more than once every two hours" into a per-window probability for a decoder that decides every four seconds: with no hold, and with three windows in a row, if windows were independent.

  2. A switch is right 95 percent of the time, deciding every four seconds. What is its information transfer rate, and what does that number leave out?

  3. Off scores are normal around 0.2 and On around 0.8, both with a spread of 0.15. The user is in On a tenth of the time, and a false switch costs five times a missed one. Where should the threshold be?

  4. Which of effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction does the light switch's verdict address?

  5. Write three testable requirements for a mind-controlled call button for someone who can't move, and how you would test each.
