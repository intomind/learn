Lab 5 · Middle school

Mind-Controlled Microcontroller

Put one program on a small board, and it switches its light by itself while your custom AI head reads On. Can it work with no computer in the room?

Get ready

You need

  • An IntoMind One with your custom AI head on it, charged, with its cable unplugged
  • A Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W. A Pico 2 without the W has no Bluetooth.
  • A micro USB cable that carries data, and a computer
  • A USB charger for the board
  • A clean microfiber, soft cotton or eyeglass cloth

Roles

Wearer
Wears the IntoMind One, and holds On or Off.
Builder
Puts the program on the board, and plugs it in.
Recorder
Writes down what the board's light does.
Skeptic
Watches: does the light follow the wearer, or something else?

Take turns, so everyone who wants to can be the wearer. In a group of three, the builder is the skeptic too.

Rules

  • Unplug the IntoMind One's cable before anyone puts it on. The board's own cable is fine.
  • Wearing it is your choice. Every role is a real one.
  • The board's pin gives only 3.3 volts: enough for an LED or a signal, and never connected to anything that plugs into a wall.
  • Wipe the electrodes between wearers with the soft cloth, since a rough one can scratch their gold coating.

Steps

  1. Put your custom AI head on

    Open the Mind-Controlled Light Switch demo, connect your IntoMind One, press Install a saved head, and choose the wearer's file. The demo puts the head on your IntoMind One, and runs it. Without a saved file, teach a new one, and run it.

    Then close the tab, so your IntoMind One is free to talk to the board, and switch it off until step 3.

    You should seeThe board drawn on the demo lighting up while the wearer holds their On thought.

  2. Put the program on the board

    Download the program from the Mind-Controlled Microcontroller guide: one file holds all of it. Hold the white BOOTSEL button on the board while you plug it into the computer, then let go. Drag the file onto the drive that appears. When the board's light blinks once a second, unplug the board until step 3.

    You should seeA drive named RP2350 that closes by itself. Then the board's light blinking once a second, while it looks for your IntoMind One.

  3. Connect your IntoMind One to the board

    PredictAfter I start my On thought, the light will come on about seconds later.

    When your teacher calls your group, plug the board back in, and switch on your IntoMind One, unplugged. The wearer puts it on where your custom AI head was taught: the back of the head, a little above the bump where the skull meets the neck. The first time, the board pairs with it, and remembers it after that.

    You should seeThe light stop blinking. Then it comes on a few seconds after you start your On thought, and goes off a few seconds after you rest.

  4. Read the light

    The board's light says what the program is doing. While you wear it, you should see only the first two kinds. The others are warnings: they are here so you can read them if they happen. Don't make them happen.

    What the light didWhat it meansDid you see it?
    One short blink a secondLooking for your IntoMind One
    Off, and onYour custom AI head reads Off, or On

    You should seeOne blink a second while the board looks, then off and on while you wear it. You may see none of the warnings, and that is a result to record too.

  5. Take the computer away

    Unplug the board from the computer, and plug it into a USB charger instead. It finds your IntoMind One by itself.

    Take turns. Before each wearer, unplug the board, so your IntoMind One is free. Put the wearer's head on it as in step 1, close the tab, and plug the board back in.

    You should seeThe light following the wearer's custom AI head, with no computer anywhere in the path.

What happened

Your custom AI head runs on your IntoMind One, not on the board. Every few seconds, the IntoMind One sends the board one number, the score: near 0 for Off, near 1 for On, and sometimes a little outside. The board compares it with 0.5. Above, it switches its light on. Below, off.

They talk over Bluetooth, with no computer between them. When they first connected, they paired: they set up secret keys, so anyone who only listens in can't read what passes between them.

The board's light could be anything its pin switches on: another LED, or a signal into a robot's own controller. That is how a brain-computer interface reaches the real world.

Talk about it

  1. Where is the decision On or Off made: on the board, or on your IntoMind One?

  2. What else could the board's pin switch on, instead of a light?

  3. Who could a board like this help?

Lab 5 · High school

Mind-Controlled Microcontroller

Program a small board to follow your custom AI head, and to switch a circuit you built. With no computer in the loop, how long does a change of mind take to reach the pin?

Background

Read it the night before.

Who does what

Your IntoMind One does the hard part. For every four seconds of your EEG, it runs the encoder and your custom AI head on its own chip, and sends one number over Bluetooth: the score, near 0 for Off and near 1 for On, and sometimes a little outside.

The board does the rest. It compares each score with 0.5, and switches its light and its pin. Once the program is on the board, no computer is needed.

The program

The program is one file of MicroPython. At its top are three settings, then two functions that are yours to change.

on_switch(on) runs when the decision changes: True for On and False for Off, and losing the link to your IntoMind One counts as Off. on_reading(score, clean) runs for every reading, with the score and whether the signal behind it was clean.

The rest of the file finds your IntoMind One, pairs with it the first time, asks it for scores, and runs the board's light, which shows what the program is doing.

Why the light lags

Each reading is four seconds of EEG, and its score comes once the reading has ended. When you switch, the reading under way holds some of each state, so its score falls in between, and the light often waits for the next reading.

So the delay depends on where in a reading you switch.

Lab 6 · College

Mind-Controlled Microcontroller

Close the loop on a board you program. How long does a change of mental state take to reach its pin, how much of that is the four-second window, and does your budget predict the rest?

Background

Read it before the lab.

The program

One MicroPython file, mind_controlled_microcontroller.py, in the demos' source. It scans for an IntoMind One, pairs the first time and reconnects with the bond after that, selects the head in slot 2, starts the stream, and turns predictions on. Each prediction carries the head's output for one four-second window: the program compares it with SWITCH_THRESHOLD, 0.5, drops any reading whose window had a gap, and counts the link lost after 20 seconds without a reading.

It is a small state machine, and the board's light shows its state. Two functions are yours: on_switch(on), when the decision changes, and on_reading(score, clean), for every reading.

A model of the delay

The IntoMind One sends a reading every four seconds. Suppose a reading's score passes 0.5 once more than half of it is On. A call that falls at a random moment in a reading is then answered when that reading ends, if the call came in its first half, or when the next one ends: between 2 and 6 seconds later, spread evenly, a mean of 4 and a standard deviation of 4/√12, about 1.15. Everything else, the model on the chip, the link, the program and the pin, adds a delay δ on top.

Timing by hand adds the people: the wearer reacting to the call, and the operator to the light.

Analyses

From the board's logs: each call's delay from the cue to the pin, the delays against the window model once the rest of the loop and the wearer's reaction are taken out, the spacing of readings, and the rate of false switches at rest with its upper bound.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def events(path, kind):
    """The board's times, in seconds, for every logged line of one kind."""
    rows = [line.split() for line in open(path) if line.startswith(kind + " ")]
    return rows, np.array([int(r[1]) / 1000 for r in rows])

_, cues = events("calls.txt", "cue")                 # the editor's log, copied to files
switches, t = events("calls.txt", "switch")
ons = t[[r[2] == "1" for r in switches]]
delays = np.array([ons[ons > c].min() - c for c in cues if (ons > c).any()])
print(f"{len(delays)} calls: median {np.median(delays):.2f} s, mean {delays.mean():.2f}, "
      f"sd {delays.std(ddof=1):.2f}")

# Against the model: uniform from 2 to 6 seconds, shifted by the rest of the loop and the
# wearer's own reaction, estimated as the mean's excess over 4.
extra = delays.mean() - 4
fit = stats.kstest(delays - extra, stats.uniform(loc=2, scale=4).cdf)
print(f"the rest of the loop and the wearer: about {extra:.2f} s; "
      f"uniform from 2 to 6 after removing it: p {fit.pvalue:.2f}")

_, readings = events("calls.txt", "reading")
gaps = np.diff(readings)
print(f"a reading every {np.median(gaps):.2f} s, the longest gap {gaps.max():.2f} s")

# At rest: switches to On while the wearer held Off, and what that rate could be.
switches, t = events("rest.txt", "switch")
_, rest_readings = events("rest.txt", "reading")
hours = (rest_readings.max() - rest_readings.min()) / 3600
n = sum(r[2] == "1" for r in switches)
upper = stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, 2 * (n + 1)) / 2 / hours           # one-sided 95 percent bound
print(f"{n} false switches in {hours * 60:.0f} minutes: "
      f"at most {upper:.0f} an hour, at 95 percent")

Open problems

  1. Evaluation in use. Delay and false switches measured in a lab, cued and watched, differ from those in a home over weeks, and long-term home use is where noninvasive interfaces are tested least (Wolpaw and others, 2018).
  2. Asynchronous control. Knowing when someone means nothing at all, the no-control state, is harder than telling two states apart, and asynchronous interfaces still trade speed for false activations (Mason and Birch, 2000).
  3. Trusting the other end. A board that can't authenticate its device, and a device that can't authenticate its heads, rest on physical control of who is nearby. How to do better on hardware this small is open.

Report

  1. The preregistration as written, and every deviation from it, each with its reason.
  2. Methods: the head and its session, the board's code, the cues, and the rest period.
  3. Results: the delays and their fit, the rate at rest and its bound.
  4. The rule you would ship for a use you name, and the threat model.
  5. The code, and the logs, which hold scores and times and nothing else.
