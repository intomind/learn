Lab 4 · Middle school

Mind-Controlled Light Switch

Can you teach a computer to tell two of your own thoughts apart, well enough to switch a light?

Get ready

You need

  • An IntoMind One, charged, with its cable unplugged
  • A computer with Chrome or Edge
  • A chair for the wearer, in a quiet spot
  • A clean microfiber, soft cotton or eyeglass cloth

Roles

Wearer
Wears the IntoMind One, and rests or counts when the demo says.
Coach
Keeps the group quiet, and keeps time.
Recorder
Writes down the score, and counts the light's switches.
Skeptic
Watches the wearer, and writes down every blink, word or jaw clench, and when.

Take turns, so everyone who wants to can be the wearer. In a group of three, the coach is the skeptic too.

Rules

  • Unplug the cable before anyone puts it on.
  • Wearing it is your choice. Every role is a real one.
  • Wipe the electrodes between wearers with the soft cloth, since a rough one can scratch their gold coating.

Steps

  1. Put it on and connect

    The IntoMind One goes on the back of the head, a little above the bump where the skull meets the neck: the same place as in Alpha Flower. Alpha, which comes from the back of the brain, usually drops when you concentrate on counting, and that change is what this lab uses.

    Unplug the cable. The wearer sits down. Put it on with the strap around the head. Part the hair under each electrode so it touches skin, and press it down until it feels firm but not tight.

    Open the Mind-Controlled Light Switch demo, press Connect your IntoMind One, and choose yours from the list.

    You should seeConnected and the battery level at the top right, and the demo's line: Off is resting, On is a thought you hold.

  2. Teach it

    Press Start teaching. For about seven minutes, the demo shows Off or On.

    Off: rest with your eyes open, and let your mind wander. On: count down from 300 by sevens, in your head. Switch when the demo switches. A high beep marks On, a low beep marks Off, and the demo says each one aloud.

    You should seeEight rounds, Off and On in turn, with the readings in each round counted under the word on the screen.

  3. Read the score

    When teaching ends, the demo teaches a custom AI head: a small model of your own that tells your two states apart. Then it tests it on readings it held back.

    You should seeA line at the bottom left, like: “Tested on readings it held back: 60 of 88 right, clearly better than guessing.” If the score isn't clearly better than guessing, the line says it could be luck, and to teach it again or pick another thought for On.

  4. Run it

    PredictThe light will follow me of 10 switches.

    Press Run it on your IntoMind One. Your custom AI head now runs on the IntoMind One's own chip, and sends a score every few seconds: near 0 for Off, near 1 for On. The page compares each score with 0.5, and shows On or Off.

    The recorder calls ten switches, On, then Off, and counts how many times the light follows.

What happened

You taught a model, as in Models that learn. Every four seconds of your EEG was one example, labeled Off or On by what the demo told you to do. On the IntoMind One's chip, a model called the encoder turns each four seconds into 76 numbers, and your custom AI head learned what makes your Off numbers different from your On numbers.

The test was fair: the readings it was tested on were ones it never learned from, and its score was compared with guessing, which gets about half of 88, or 44.

The light changes a few seconds after you do, because each reading is four seconds of EEG. Your custom AI head never knows what you were thinking. It only learned what made two of your states look different.

  3. The skeptic wrote down blinks and jaw clenches. Could those have fooled the custom AI head? How would you check?

Lab 4 · High school

Mind-Controlled Light Switch

Choose two mental states, and the place on the head where they should differ most. Can a model learn to tell them apart, and how will you know it isn't luck?

Background

Read it the night before.

Teaching a model your states

The demo teaches a classifier from labeled examples. Each example is four seconds of your EEG, summed up as 76 numbers by the IntoMind One's encoder, and labeled Off while you rest and On while you hold your On state. It collects them in eight rounds, Off and On in turn, eleven readings in each.

The model it fits is your custom AI head. To test it fairly, the demo teaches it eight times, each time holding back one round and testing on it, so each of the 88 readings is scored by a head that never learned from it. A whole round is held back, not single readings, because readings close together in time are alike.

Better than guessing?

With two states taught equally, guessing gets about half right: 44 of 88 readings. A score is evidence only when it is clearly above that, and the demo works out how far above is clear, by teaching and testing a fresh head on each of the 70 ways to relabel the rounds.

Blinks, talking and a clenched jaw make large signals that aren't from the brain. If one state has more of them, they can raise the score for the wrong reason.

Choose your states, and where they show

Each mental state changes the signal most over one part of the brain, so you choose your two states and the site together.

A fist, made or just imagined, changes the mu rhythm over the motor cortex on the opposite side of the brain from the hand. So a right fist shows at C3, on the left, about three fifths of the way from the left ear up to the top of the head.

Lab 5 · College

Mind-Controlled Light Switch

Choose two mental states and the site where they should differ most. Can a regularized linear model on the encoder's embeddings tell them apart on rounds it never saw, beyond what relabeling the rounds gives, and does it still work at the mirror site?

Background

Read it before the lab.

The pipeline

Every four seconds the encoder turns the EEG into 76 numbers. While you teach, the demo labels each embedding Off or On by the cue on screen, drops the two readings after every switch, which still carry the old state, and drops any reading whose four seconds had a gap. Eight rounds, Off and On in turn, keep eleven readings each: 88.

Your browser fits ridge regression from the 76 numbers to the labels, with λ from the embeddings alone, scores it by holding out each round in turn, rounds the weights to bytes, and installs the custom AI head on the IntoMind One, which sends a score every four seconds. The page, or a board, compares it with 0.5.

States and sites

Each state changes the signal most over one part of the cortex, so you choose the pair and the site together. A fist, made or imagined, desynchronizes the mu rhythm over the motor cortex opposite the hand: C3 for the right hand. Taught at C3, a head that learned one hemisphere's change should fall toward chance at the mirror site, C4.

Against chance

Judged as 88 independent tries, a one-sided binomial test draws its 0.05 line at 53 right. But readings in a round share a state, a posture and a stretch of time, so the binomial overstates the evidence. The demo instead scores every one of the C(8, 4) = 70 ways to call four rounds On, with the same folds. Since a labeling and its mirror score alike, p can't go below 2/70, and the demo calls a score clearly better than guessing when p is below 0.05.

Analysis

First, reproduce one head from its training data with the demos' Python program, and check it matches the one the browser saved, byte for byte. Then, on your own training data, measure what random folds would have claimed, and run the permutation test over rounds yourself.

import json
from itertools import combinations
import numpy as np

data = json.load(open("training-data.json"))      # the training data the demo saved
X = np.array([w["embedding"] for w in data["windows"]], float)
y = np.array([w["label"] for w in data["windows"]], float)
round_of = np.array([w["round"] for w in data["windows"]])

def fit(X, y):
    """Ridge with the trainer's λ, from the embeddings alone, and no penalty on the intercept."""
    Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)
    ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Xc.T @ Xc)
    lam = np.sqrt(max(ev[0], 1) * ev[1])
    A = np.c_[X, np.ones(len(X))]
    P = lam * np.eye(A.shape[1])
    P[-1, -1] = 0
    return np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + P, A.T @ y)

def right(X, y, folds):
    """Readings right when each fold is held out in turn."""
    n = 0
    for test in folds:
        train = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(y)), test)
        w = fit(X[train], y[train])
        n += np.sum((np.c_[X[test], np.ones(len(test))] @ w > 0.5) == y[test])
    return int(n)

rounds = np.unique(round_of)
by_round = [np.flatnonzero(round_of == r) for r in rounds]
shuffled = np.array_split(np.random.default_rng(0).permutation(len(y)), len(rounds))
real = right(X, y, by_round)
print("random folds:", right(X, y, shuffled), "of", len(y))
print("a round held out at a time:", real, "of", len(y))

# Every way of calling as many rounds On as were taught, scored the same way.
taught_on = [r for r in rounds if y[round_of == r][0] == 1]
null = [right(X, np.isin(round_of, on).astype(float), by_round)
        for on in combinations(rounds, len(taught_on))]
print("p over", len(null), "labelings:", round(np.mean(np.array(null) >= real), 3))

Lab 5 · Graduate

Mind-Controlled Light Switch

Can a linear head on the encoder's embeddings tell two of your states apart on rounds it never saw, does the demo's test of that hold up, and does the head still work on another day?

The demo, exactly

PartWhat it doesSource
TeachingEight rounds, Off and On in turn, starting with Off, cued by a word and a beep, high for On. Each keeps 11 embeddings, one every four seconds, after dropping the 2 after each switch and any with a gap in the signal: 88 in about seven minutes.light-switch/main.js
The fitRidge regression of the labels, 0 and 1, on the embeddings as integers, the intercept unpenalized, its penalty the geometric mean of the two smallest eigenvalues of the centered Gram matrix. Refused unless there are more embeddings than 76.lib/train.js
The headWeights rounded to signed bytes against the largest, the bias in the same units: 156 bytes, installed in slot 2.lib/train.js
The scoreEight folds of one round each. Every labeling of four rounds On, 70 in all, scored with the same folds by heads rounded as the device runs them. Clearly better than guessing when the share scoring at least as well is under 0.05, which can't fall below 2/70.lib/train.js
RunningThe device scores every window, and the light follows each score above or below 0.5. A window with a gap leaves it as it was.light-switch/main.js
SavingThe head, and the training data: every kept embedding with its round and label, the states and the encoder's identity, as JSON. The Python program teach_it.py rebuilds the same head from it, byte for byte.lib/train.js, python/teach_it.py

What it leaves out: any second day, since a head is taught and tested within one session. A penalty chosen for prediction rather than for stability, with 77 windows to fit 76 weights in every fold. And any check on what the head learned: blinks, muscle and posture that change with the state are as learnable as the state.

States and sites

Each state changes the signal most over one part of the cortex, so the pair and the site are chosen together. A fist, made or imagined, desynchronizes the mu and beta rhythms over the motor cortex opposite the hand (Pfurtscheller and Lopes da Silva, 1999): C3 for the right hand. Taught at C3, a head that learned one hemisphere's change should fall toward chance at the mirror site, C4. Inner speech is weak in EEG, and decoding it sits near chance (Panachakel and Ramakrishnan, 2021).

Between days the embeddings move: contact, posture, alertness and the electrodes' exact place all change, and a head fitted on one session meets a shifted distribution on the next (Shenoy and others, 2006). Removing each session's mean before fitting and applying is the simplest correction, a Euclidean cousin of re-centering covariance matrices (Zanini and others, 2018).

import json
from itertools import combinations
import numpy as np

def load(path):
    """A training file the demo saved: embeddings as integers, labels, and rounds."""
    w = json.load(open(path))["windows"]
    X = np.array([v["embedding"] for v in w], float)
    return X, np.array([v["label"] for v in w], float), np.array([v["round"] for v in w])

def penalty(X, rule):
    """Both use no labels: "gap" is the demo's, "mean" the Gram matrix's mean eigenvalue."""
    ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh((X - X.mean(0)).T @ (X - X.mean(0)))
    return np.sqrt(max(ev[0], 1) * ev[1]) if rule == "gap" else ev.mean()

def fit(X, y, rule):
    """Ridge with the intercept unpenalized: the weights and the intercept."""
    m = X.mean(0)
    xc = X - m
    lam = penalty(X, rule)
    w = np.linalg.solve(xc.T @ xc + lam * np.eye(X.shape[1]), xc.T @ (y - y.mean()))
    return w, y.mean() - m @ w

def held_out(X, y, r, rule):
    """Readings right with each round held out in turn."""
    right = 0
    for k in np.unique(r):
        w, b = fit(X[r != k], y[r != k], rule)
        right += np.sum((X[r == k] @ w + b > 0.5) == (y[r == k] == 1))
    return right

def relabelings(y, r):
    """Every labeling of the rounds with as many On as were taught: 70 for 4 of 8."""
    rounds = np.unique(r)
    on = sum(y[r == k][0] for k in rounds)
    return [np.isin(r, c).astype(float) for c in combinations(rounds, int(on))]

s1, s2 = load("w1-session1.json"), load("w1-session2.json")     # the files you saved
for rule in ("gap", "mean"):
    for name, (X, y, r) in (("session 1", s1), ("session 2", s2)):
        real = held_out(X, y, r, rule)
        p = np.mean([held_out(X, z, r, rule) >= real for z in relabelings(y, r)])
        print(f"{rule:4s} {name}: {real} of {len(y)} on held-out rounds, p {p:.3f}")
    # Transfer: the head from all of session 1, on all of session 2, against its relabelings.
    for recenter in (False, True):
        (X1, y1, _), (X2, y2, r2) = s1, s2
        if recenter:                        # each session's own mean, which uses no labels
            X1, X2 = X1 - X1.mean(0), X2 - X2.mean(0)
        w, b = fit(X1, y1, rule)
        hit = lambda z: np.sum((X2 @ w + b > 0.5) == (z == 1))
        p = np.mean([hit(z) >= hit(y2) for z in relabelings(y2, r2)])
        print(f"{rule:4s} session 1's head on session 2{', recentered' if recenter else ''}: "
              f"{hit(y2)} of {len(y2)}, p {p:.3f}")

(X1, y1, _), (X2, y2, _) = s1, s2
shift = np.linalg.norm(X2.mean(0) - X1.mean(0))
states = np.linalg.norm(X1[y1 == 1].mean(0) - X1[y1 == 0].mean(0))
print(f"the sessions' means are {shift:.0f} apart, "
      f"the two states' means within session 1 {states:.0f}")

Open problems

  1. Another day. Nonstationarity between sessions is among the main obstacles to everyday use of noninvasive interfaces (Shenoy and others, 2006). How to keep a head working without teaching it again is open.
  2. Who can't. For some people a decoder never separates the states, which has been called BCI illiteracy (Vidaurre and Blankertz, 2010). Thompson (2019) argues the term puts on the person a failure of the method, and how to design for everyone is open.
  3. The body in the signal. Telling a state from the blinks, muscle and posture that come with it, without many channels or other sensors, is unsolved, and a high score doesn't settle it.
